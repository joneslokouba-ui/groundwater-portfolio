# Contaminant Transport in Groundwater — Core Mechanisms

Groundwater contaminants move through an aquifer via a combination of
physical, chemical, and biological processes. Understanding which
process dominates at a site determines both how a plume will evolve
and which remediation strategy makes sense.

## Advection
Advection is the bulk movement of dissolved contaminant carried along
with flowing groundwater, driven by the hydraulic gradient and
controlled by the aquifer's hydraulic conductivity and effective
porosity. Advective velocity sets the baseline speed at which a plume
migrates downgradient, independent of any spreading or attenuation.

## Dispersion
As groundwater moves through the pore network, small-scale variations
in flow path length and velocity cause the contaminant mass to spread
out both along the direction of flow (longitudinal dispersion) and
perpendicular to it (transverse dispersion). Dispersion dilutes peak
concentrations over time even without any mass loss, and is typically
represented with a dispersivity coefficient scaled to the length of
the flow path being modeled.

## Sorption and Retardation
Many contaminants partition between the dissolved (mobile) phase and
the solid aquifer matrix (immobile phase) through sorption — commonly
described by a distribution coefficient (Kd) for a linear, reversible
sorption model. Sorption slows the apparent velocity of the
contaminant relative to groundwater flow, an effect quantified by a
retardation factor. Highly sorptive compounds (e.g., many chlorinated
solvents' degradation products, certain metals) travel much slower
than the groundwater itself and can act as a long-term source even
after the original release is controlled.

## Degradation and Attenuation
Contaminants can also be destroyed or transformed in place through
biological degradation (microbial metabolism), abiotic chemical
reactions (hydrolysis, redox transformation), or radioactive decay
for certain isotopes. When these destructive processes combine with
dilution and sorption to reduce contaminant mass and concentration
over time without engineered intervention, the combined effect is
referred to as natural attenuation.

## Why This Matters for Site Management
A conceptual site model should identify which of these processes is
dominant before selecting a remediation technology — for example, a
highly sorptive, slowly degrading contaminant in a low-permeability
aquifer behaves very differently from a mobile, readily biodegradable
compound in a high-permeability sand aquifer, even if both are
released at similar concentrations.
