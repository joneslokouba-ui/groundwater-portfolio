# Monitored Natural Attenuation (MNA)

Monitored Natural Attenuation is a remediation strategy that relies on
naturally occurring physical, chemical, and biological processes to
reduce contaminant mass, concentration, and toxicity over time,
combined with a long-term monitoring program to confirm the plume is
actually shrinking or stable rather than continuing to expand.

## When MNA Is Appropriate
MNA is generally considered where:
- Site risk assessment shows no unacceptable exposure pathway to
  receptors during the time it takes for attenuation to occur.
- There is a well-documented source area that is either removed or
  controlled, since MNA manages the existing dissolved plume rather
  than an ongoing uncontrolled release.
- Lines of evidence support that attenuation processes are actively
  occurring — not just that concentrations happen to be low.

## Lines of Evidence
A defensible MNA case typically draws on three types of evidence:
1. **Historical trend analysis** — statistically declining
   concentrations and/or a stable or shrinking plume footprint over
   multiple monitoring rounds.
2. **Geochemical indicators** — depletion of electron acceptors
   (dissolved oxygen, nitrate, sulfate) and/or accumulation of
   degradation byproducts (e.g., daughter products of chlorinated
   solvent degradation, methane, ferrous iron) consistent with
   biodegradation actually occurring along the flow path.
3. **Microbiological evidence** — presence and activity of
   contaminant-degrading microbial populations, sometimes confirmed
   through molecular biological tools.

## Common Misapplication
MNA is sometimes proposed simply because a site is difficult or
expensive to remediate actively — but declining concentrations from
dilution or sampling variability alone are not the same as
destructive attenuation. A rigorous MNA case needs geochemical and/or
microbiological evidence that mass is genuinely being destroyed or
immobilized, not just diluted below a detection limit temporarily.

## Long-Term Monitoring
Because MNA relies on ongoing natural processes rather than an
engineered fix, sites typically require a monitoring program spanning
years to decades, with contingency triggers defined in advance (e.g.,
if concentrations increase or the plume expands beyond a compliance
boundary, an active remediation technology is implemented instead).
