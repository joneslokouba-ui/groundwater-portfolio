# Bioremediation of Groundwater Contaminants

Bioremediation uses microbial metabolism to transform or destroy
contaminants, either by stimulating naturally occurring microbial
populations (biostimulation) or by introducing specialized organisms
capable of degrading the target compound (bioaugmentation).

## Aerobic vs. Anaerobic Pathways
- **Aerobic bioremediation** relies on oxygen as the electron
  acceptor and is generally effective for petroleum hydrocarbons
  (BTEX compounds, straight-chain alkanes), which microbes can use
  directly as a carbon and energy source.
- **Anaerobic bioremediation** uses alternative electron acceptors
  (nitrate, sulfate, iron, or the contaminant itself) and is the
  dominant pathway for reductive dechlorination of chlorinated
  solvents such as PCE and TCE, which are broken down stepwise into
  daughter products (TCE to cis-DCE to vinyl chloride to ethene)
  under sufficiently reducing conditions.

## Biostimulation
Biostimulation adds a substrate (an electron donor such as lactate,
vegetable oil, or molasses, or an electron acceptor such as oxygen
via air sparging) to encourage the existing native microbial
population to increase its degradation rate. This is often the
first-line approach where the necessary microbial populations are
already present but limited by a lack of an appropriate donor or
acceptor.

## Bioaugmentation
Where the necessary degrading microbial populations are absent or
present at very low abundance, bioaugmentation introduces cultured
microbial consortia known to perform the needed transformation — for
example, Dehalococcoides species are commonly bioaugmented at
chlorinated solvent sites because they are the only organisms known
to completely dechlorinate vinyl chloride to the non-toxic end
product, ethene.

## Key Design Considerations
- **Delivery method**: injection wells, recirculation systems, or
  permeable reactive barriers determine how evenly the amendment
  reaches the contaminated zone.
- **Geochemical monitoring**: tracking electron donor/acceptor
  concentrations, oxidation-reduction potential, and daughter product
  formation confirms the intended biological process is actually
  occurring, not just that the amendment was delivered.
- **Rebound risk**: if treatment is stopped before the source area is
  sufficiently depleted, concentrations can rebound as residual mass
  desorbs back into the dissolved phase.

## Intrinsic vs. Engineered Bioremediation
"Intrinsic" bioremediation refers to biodegradation occurring without
any engineered enhancement — effectively the biological component of
natural attenuation. Engineered bioremediation (biostimulation and/or
bioaugmentation) is used when intrinsic rates are judged too slow to
meet site cleanup timelines or regulatory requirements on their own.
