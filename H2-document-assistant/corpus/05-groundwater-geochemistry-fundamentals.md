# Groundwater Geochemistry Fundamentals for Contamination Assessment

Geochemical conditions in an aquifer control which contaminants are
mobile, which are retained on the solid matrix, and which
biodegradation pathways are thermodynamically favorable — making
basic geochemistry a prerequisite for interpreting a contaminant
plume correctly.

## Redox Conditions
Aquifers exist along a spectrum from oxidizing to strongly reducing,
and microbial communities use electron acceptors in a predictable
thermodynamic sequence as oxygen is depleted: dissolved oxygen first,
then nitrate, then manganese and iron oxides, then sulfate, and
finally carbon dioxide (methanogenesis) under the most reducing
conditions. Measuring this sequence of electron acceptor depletion
and byproduct accumulation (nitrite, dissolved manganese/iron,
sulfide, methane) is one of the main ways to determine what redox
zone a given part of a plume sits in, and therefore which
degradation pathways are geochemically possible there.

## pH and Mineral Equilibria
Groundwater pH controls the solubility of many metals and the
stability of mineral phases that can either sequester or release
contaminants. Chemical oxidation and reduction reactions used in
remediation (e.g., ISCO with permanganate) can shift local pH and
redox conditions significantly, sometimes mobilizing naturally
occurring metals (such as arsenic bound to iron oxides) as an
unintended side effect if not accounted for in the design.

## Sorption and the Role of Aquifer Mineralogy
The tendency of a contaminant to sorb onto aquifer solids depends on
both the contaminant's chemistry (hydrophobicity, charge) and the
mineralogy of the aquifer matrix — organic carbon content strongly
controls sorption of many organic contaminants, while clay minerals
and iron/manganese oxide coatings control sorption of metals and some
polar organics. This is why the same contaminant can behave very
differently — mobile in a clean sand aquifer, strongly retarded in an
aquifer with higher organic carbon or clay content.

## Why Geochemistry Drives Remediation Design
A remediation technology that works by exploiting or altering redox
conditions (bioremediation, ISCO, ISCR, permeable reactive barriers)
can only be designed correctly with a baseline geochemical
characterization — without it, an engineered fix risks working
against the aquifer's natural chemistry rather than with it, or
producing unintended secondary effects like metals mobilization.
